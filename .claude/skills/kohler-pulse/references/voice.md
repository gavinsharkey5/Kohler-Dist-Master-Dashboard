# Voice

Every item is **headline → what we are seeing → why it matters**. Never
headline → who is responsible → what they failed to do.

The Pulse should make an experienced sales leader curious, not informed of a
verdict. He should read a line and start thinking *why is that happening?*,
*is that what I'm seeing out there?*, *which manager do I ask?* Leave him the
last step. He has forty years of instinct; the report feeds it rather than
replacing it.

Write like a knowledgeable colleague catching him in the hallway. Short
sentences. Numbers support the story; they are not the story.

## Rewrites that were asked for

These are real edits from real feedback. The pattern generalises.

Also retired: "that makes it a manager conversation", "very different
execution", "this one lives with those five", "before we start scoring anyone".

## Naming people

**Positive recognition is specific. Negative analysis is general.**

Good: "Powierski, Melo, Barreca, Payton, Ehlers and Lagala are all close to
completion." That is recognition and it reads well.

Not good: listing the five reps who are behind with their target counts. Say
"most of the remaining opportunity is concentrated with a small group of
on-premise reps" and let managers find the names in the dashboard. The Pulse
surfaces the opportunity; the dashboard holds the accountability.

The exception is a genuine emergency needing intervention this week — rare, and
worth checking with the user first.

## Length

One headline, one to three short sentences. If an item needs a fourth sentence,
the detail belongs in the dashboard.

Compare — same finding, two altitudes:

> **Too much.** "Nick Melissari (134 target accounts), Robin Feldman (99), Paul
> McLaughlin (97), Brian Sengebush (96) and Allison Scott (80) hold 506 of the
> 656 targets. Only Sengebush has hit the two-line goal."

> **Right.** "Most of the available targets sit with a small group of
> on-premise reps, and very little of that distribution has been written this
> month. Because the opportunity is concentrated, even a modest pickup could
> change the month-end result."

The second took the same analysis and spent none of his attention on arithmetic.

## Distinguish the causes

Not every gap is execution. Before framing something as a miss, work out which
of these it actually is — and say so:

execution · lack of opportunity · inventory constraint · portfolio limitation ·
normal fluctuation · a real performance problem

Most months contain at most one of the last.

## Headline test

Would a VP of Sales care enough to mention this to a manager? If it only says
somebody is behind, rewrite it or cut it.
